The Lionfish Invasion

An urgent threat to Atlantic marine ecosystems

Overview of the Crisis

Native to the Indo-Pacific, lionfish were introduced to the Atlantic Ocean in the late 20th century. With no natural predators in these new waters, their populations have exploded. They are ravenous predators that consume native fish at alarming rates, disrupting local food chains and destroying fragile coral reef environments.

They possess 18 venomous spines along their backs for defense, making them dangerous to both marine life and human divers who attempt to harvest them.

Immediate intervention is required to manage their rapid spread throughout the Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico.

Why Are They Winning?

Lionfish possess unique biological advantages that allow them to outcompete local species:

  • Rapid Reproduction: A single female can release up to 2 million eggs per year.
  • Indiscriminate Eating: They can expand their stomachs up to 30 times its normal volume to gorge on prey.
  • Camouflage: Native prey do not recognize them as predators due to their unusual stripes and fans.
